## 1. Tiled roof (青瓦 / 黑瓦)

**Master/tutorial simplification.**
- Tutorial order for a tile house: front roof as a rough triangle/trapezoid with tile lines; then the side plane
  in light flowing strokes; then beams/pillars under the eave, *varying their length* to open space; then steps.
  Precision is explicitly not the goal. [OPENED S1]
- Two named ways to do tiles in a water-town picture: "straight pulling" (平拉直线法, a row of parallel pulled
  lines) and "splash ink" (泼墨法, a loaded mass). Roofs come *before* walls in the order. [OPENED S2]
- Wu Guanzhong-type reduction: the roof is a dark block, the wall a white block; detail is a few lines. [OPENED S5, S10]
  A Chinese snippet says three or four strokes (an inverted-V plus verticals) make white wall + dark roof
  [SNIPPET, search result for Wu query]; treat as plausible, unverified.
- Anatomy worth keeping: main ridge horizontal at top; tile courses run **down the slope in parallel with rafters**
  (i.e. lines follow the fall line, not horizontal hatching); eave line curves gently up at the ends;
  gable triangle shows on side of hip-gable roofs. [OPENED S11]

**Practical recipe for our engine** [DERIVED from above + MEMORY of how xieyi roofs look]:
1. Roof silhouette first as ONE filled shape (ridge line slightly longer than the wall below; eave hangs past wall
   by ~8-15% of wall width; ends of eave lift a little). Fill with mid-dark wash, wet-in-wet, darker toward the
   eave (shadow under eave) - this is the "mass" that reads as roof.
2. Tile courses: 4-8 lines per roof plane at any scale, *pulled from ridge toward eave*, broken/dry (飞白), uneven
   length, NOT evenly spaced, NOT spanning the whole plane. Stop some short; let some lines start mid-slope.
   Suggest, do not tabulate.
3. Ridge: one darker, slightly thicker line, with tiny upturn marks at ends if the building is big enough.
4. Eave shadow: a thin dark band directly under the roof edge, then a pale wall. This band is what separates roof
   from wall; without it both merge.
5. Far/small roofs (<~40 px wide): no courses at all, only mass + one ridge stroke + eave shadow.

**Proportions.** Roof is visually 1/3 to 1/2 of a house's height in Jiangnan houses; dark roof area usually
larger than door/window dark area. [MEMORY]

**Left as paper:** the wall below, the sky around the roof edge, tile gaps (flying white inside strokes).
**Depth/scale:** nearer roofs get more courses, sharper darker edge, stronger eave shadow; farther roofs are paler,
softer, fewer marks ("near dense/dark, far pale/void"). [OPENED S2 gives the near-far rule for bridges/boats;
applying it to roofs is DERIVED]

**Beginner mistakes.** Barcode hatching = many evenly spaced, equal-length, equal-dark lines across the whole
roof (what the tests produced). Also: drawing tiles horizontally; making the roof a flat stripe with no eave
overhang; uniform tone.
**False-word risks.** Dense parallel lines read as barcode, wood grain, a grille or a basket. Rolled ridge + no
eave reads as a hat or a box lid. Dark trapezoid with no ridge reads as a tent or a book.

## 2. White-walled houses (粉墙黛瓦)
- Wall is paper. Wu's pictures keep large *blank* wall areas against dark tile; the white is the main design,
  the drawn marks are windows, roof, and staining. [OPENED S10, S8]
- Tutorial route for walls: place walls with light ink, then darker tone for effect, then blend wet so the edge
  diffuses; one named alternative is to use splash ink + leave white for the exterior wall. [OPENED S2]
- Practical [DERIVED/MEMORY]: no outline on the white side that faces light; define a wall by the dark roof above,
  the dark windows/doors, a base line (dark irregular at the ground), and one or two thin pale-grey stains running
  down from the eave. Shaded side wall = one flat pale-grey wash with wet edge. Windows: small dark rectangles,
  varied size, 2-4 per wall, not a grid.
- Mistakes: outlining every wall in black (becomes a cardboard box); filling walls grey (loses 粉墙 brightness);
  identical windows. False words: a fully outlined white rectangle = a sheet of paper / a sugar cube.

## 3. Umbrella in rain (hardest, least supported)
**What sources give:** umbrellas (and straw cloaks, wind-bent trees) are a way to *signal rain without painting
rain* - the "indirect" method. [OPENED S3]. Lu Chen's 《清明》 (walkers with umbrellas in qingming rain) is cited
as a classic umbrella figure subject [SNIPPET, not opened]. Feng Zikai draws figures in very few strokes, often
faceless, with the meaning in posture [SNIPPET]. **No tutorial for the umbrella shape itself was found.**
Only transferable tool: the graded stroke - load the brush so a single stroke goes dark to pale (angled dip, or
one side dark) - and "flying white" from a fast dry sweep. [OPENED S13]

**Recipe** [MEMORY + DERIVED]:
1. Umbrella canopy = ONE wide broad side-brush stroke (a shallow arc, flat top edge slightly bowed; width about
   2.5-3.5x shoulder width), darker at the top rim, fading toward the lower edge. NOT a filled semicircle with
   outline, NOT a pie with spokes. Leave the lower edge ragged/dry so it reads as a cloth skirt.
2. One thin handle/shaft line, slightly off-centre, visible only below the canopy down to the hand.
3. Body under it: a narrow vertical dark mass (robe) + tiny head dot slightly offset and partly hidden by
   canopy; 1-2 short leg strokes or none (feet dissolve into a wet ground reflection).
4. Tilt: canopy leans slightly into the wind; shaft follows the tilt. Varying tilt between people is the whole
   crowd feeling.
5. Seen from the side not top: if from above, see section 8.
6. Colour in a monochrome picture: tonal variation only (one pale, one mid, one dark canopy per group), no
   patterns.
**Left as paper:** ground around feet (with a pale grey reflection smudge), the space between canopy and body
edge.
**Mistakes seen in tests:** flat discs (full outline + uniform fill), pies (radial spokes), perfect symmetry,
identical size/tone.
**False-word risks:** a disc on a stick reads as a mushroom, a lollipop, a flag, a tree. A dome with spokes reads
as a pie or a jellyfish. A dark dome alone with no body reads as a beetle/stone. Mitigation: always a body and a
hint of ground wetness under it.

## 4. Crowds (人群)
**Sources:** in landscape, figures are deliberately small; in Song and later they shrink into 点景 with simple
activities and in Ming-Qing sometimes a few rough strokes. [OPENED S9] Painters' rules of thumb quoted in
tutorials: "a mountain by the zhang, a tree by the chi, a horse by the cun, a person by the fen" (relative scale).
[OPENED S1] Density follows distance and place: rules attributed to Wang Wei that a ferry landing should be quiet
and people sparse [SNIPPET, quote via search result, not verified]. Zhang Zeduan's Qingming scroll: crowd
varies from empty to dense, people cluster around an *event* (boat passing the bridge), many are turned to
each other [SNIPPET; page not opened].
**Recipe** [MEMORY + DERIVED]:
1. Do not scatter independent individuals. Make 3-5 *clusters* of 2-7 and a few loners; vary cluster spacing
   (tight cluster, gap, looser cluster). Clusters overlap: back figures show only head and shoulders.
2. Per figure 2-4 marks: a head dot, a body stroke (vertical wedge, wider at the bottom), optionally a hat or
   carried pole. Height varies +/-15%; adults:children ~1:0.7.
3. Tone: front figures darker with crisp edge, back figures paler and wetter; heads are the darkest dots (hair/hats)
   so a crowd reads as a *line of dots over a pale body band*.
4. Facing and lean vary; a few get a pole, basket or bundle - these silhouettes break the pebble look.
5. Keep a sliver of paper between most figures; let overlaps merge into one mass in the far field.
6. Far crowd (<8 px per person): stop drawing people, paint a textured band of dots over a pale wash.
**Mistakes:** identical pebbles (same size, same tone, same shape, even spacing). **False words:** a row of
equal dark blobs reads as a pebble beach, a string of beads, a tree line, or bird droppings on the page.

## 5. Stalls and awnings
**No source found.** [MEMORY/DERIVED]: awning = one slanted quick band (a flat, slightly sagging parallelogram),
mid-dark, darker at the front edge and often with a dry hanging edge; 1-2 posts (thin verticals) and a counter
as a dark horizontal; goods as a few dots/short strokes in a row (vary size); a figure or two behind the counter,
half hidden. Stalls shelter *under* the roofs of the street, so keep the awning pale compared to the tile roof.
Mistakes: outlined boxes with stripes (becomes a circus tent / gift box); identical stalls in a row.
False words: slanted dark band alone = a roof, a book or a bridge deck; give it posts and goods.

## 6. Bridges
- Bridges are valued for connecting separate zones and leading the eye through depth; often done "in a few
  strokes" with a farmer/donkey/fisherman under or over them. [OPENED S6]
- Order in the water-town tutorial: sketch houses, then bridge, then roofs, walls, boats, water; ink strength drops
  with distance ("near big, far small, near solid, far void, near dark, far pale"). [OPENED S2]
- Wu Guanzhong values the stone arch for its geometry: round arch, square blocks, curved back. [OPENED S10]
- A general snippet says stone arch bridges are done arch first, then railings, then wall texture, with the
  arch's dark inner face symmetrical [SNIPPET, sketch-tutorial, not opened].
**Recipe** [DERIVED]: stone arch: the bridge back = one curved dark stroke (thicker), span opening = a darker
half-ellipse (inner shadow) with its reflection below completing the ellipse in pale grey - reflection + opening
together form the ring that says "arch". Railing = 3-5 short vertical ticks, not a fence. Steps = 3-4 offset short
strokes at the ends. Figures on the bridge: 2-3 tiny dots-with-body. Plank bridge: a single slightly bowed line
with 2 leg strokes.
**Mistakes:** drawing every stone; a perfect semi-circle; bridge as a flat grey rectangle. **False words:** a
thick curved line = a rainbow, a handle, a croquet hoop; unsupported arch + no banks = floating arc. Always end it
into bank shapes.

## 7. Figures, scale, and proportion
- Keep the body to head, torso, limbs and hat/cloak cues; faces may be omitted entirely. [SNIPPET, landscape
  figure tutorial, not opened; consistent with S9]
- Boats/figures: two sources [OPENED S7, weaker] suggest ordering: hull centre line first, then person, then
  awning, then hull outline; don't over-detail (over-fine = stiff).
- Proportion rule above [OPENED S1]. [MEMORY] typical 点景 figure is 1/60-1/30 of picture height in a full
  landscape; larger in a street close-up (1/12-1/8).

## 8. Figures seen from above
**No source found.** [DERIVED] From high vantage the canopy/hat is the main shape and the body mostly vanishes.
Person = head dot + two shoulder smudges (a short wide stroke) + tiny feet hint. Umbrella from above = a round
dark mass, but give it rim irregularity and a pale rim ring; overlap neighbours so it becomes a textured field
of overlapping dark blobs; let gaps show the wet ground in pale grey. Real risk: a field of circles = polka dots
or mushrooms; break with shape variety (rect market cloth, carried goods), tone variety, and diagonal flow
lines (the current of the crowd).

## 9. Town / street composition (black roof, white wall rhythm)
- Wu Guanzhong's water towns: black/white/grey as the base; the design is interlocking planes of black roof and
  white wall with dots (people, laundry, blossom) and lines (willow, wires, stairs, water edge). [OPENED S4, S5, S8, S10]
  "Beauty lies in dense arrangement and staggered variation" (his idea, paraphrased from S10).
- Dense architecture is balanced with open water/sky; narrow stepped streets and bridges act as paths through
  the picture. [OPENED S8]
- Tutorial: building is the subject; set out the building type and placement first, use level-far and
  deep-far (平远/深远) layering for depth. [OPENED S2]; composition rule "sparse enough to run a horse, dense
  enough that no wind passes" [OPENED S12].
**How to plan it** [DERIVED from the above]:
1. Decide the void first: a large pale sky/water/wall region = 40-60% of the frame. Do not fill everything.
2. Roof/wall rhythm: alternate dark-mass / white-mass with unequal widths (e.g. 3:1:2:5), staggered in height,
   eaves stepping, a few steep gables as accents. Never two equal roofs side by side.
3. One dense cluster (the place the eye lands: bridge, stall row, crowd) near a third point; one near-black
   small accent there (darkest value in the picture lives here).
4. Eye path: enter at bottom-left via a bank or stair line -> water/road curve -> bridge -> cluster -> ridge
   line -> up to sky void. A reflected roof line in water repeats the rhythm in pale.
5. Depth: near houses crisp/dark/large with windows; middle ones mass + ridge; far ones pale grey wedges fading
   into mist. Mist bands between layers are the separator, not outlines.
6. Dots and lines over planes: a few figure dots along the road, willow lines breaking a white wall, wires.

## 10. Rain and mist around dense subjects
- Three documented routes: (a) direct - many shallow strokes of pale ink, made with big broken-tip quick sweeps
  (Fu Baoshi), or white powder flicked on pale ink (Liu Cunhui); (b) indirect - umbrellas, straw cloaks, wind-bent
  trees and reeds; (c) atmosphere - damp, rising mist, blurred edges, no visible rain (Mi Fu/Xia Gui tradition). [OPENED S3]
- For dense subjects (town) prefer (c)+(b): streaks across the dense part make it a mess. [DERIVED]
  Recipe: wet the far and mid layers so edges bleed; keep the foreground crisp; pale grey band across the
  middle; roofs lose their lines first (far = flat grey), white walls get a cool grey wash; a few diagonal
  streaks only in the sky void or over water, all parallel, same lean as the umbrellas; ground and water
  glisten as pale reflection smears under figures. Umbrellas lean with the wind; wet stone = small dark
  horizontal dashes plus pale vertical reflection.
- Mistake: rain lines over the darkest detail (muddy); uniform mist (flat fog overlay) instead of layered bands.
- False words: over-regular diagonal streaks read as scratches or hatching; too-flat mist reads as a dirty lens.
